# Thai Festival Cards, Greetings & Condolences

## Overview

A greeting or gift suggestion that's fine in a Western context can be a real misstep in a Thai one — a literally-translated "happy new year, here's a clock as a gift" idea, or choosing black for a birthday card, carries unintended associations a model without this skill won't catch. This skill covers the festival-specific phrasing and the taboo list (numbers, colors, gift items) that apply across greeting-card contexts.

## When to use

- เขียนคำอวยพรปีใหม่, สงกรานต์, ลอยกระทง
- เขียนคำไว้อาลัย / การ์ดแสดงความเสียใจ
- เขียนการ์ดอวยพรงานแต่งงาน
- ตรวจสอบว่าตัวเลข/สี/ของขวัญที่เลือกมีความหมายไม่ดีในบริบทไทยหรือไม่

## When NOT to use

- Purely secular, non-Thai-context greetings — general translation is enough
- Business gift-giving with a specific corporate policy already defined — follow the company's own policy over this skill's general guidance

## Core knowledge

**Songkran (สงกรานต์, mid-April, Thai New Year)**: water festival, but the deeper tradition is รดน้ำดำหัว — pouring scented water over elders' hands as a sign of respect and asking forgiveness/blessing. A greeting card can reference either the festive water-play side or the respect-for-elders side depending on the audience (younger/peer audience → festive tone; addressed to an elder → respect-forward tone, closer to the register used in `thai-government-form`'s formal salutation guidance).

**Loy Krathong (ลอยกระทง, full moon of the 12th lunar month, usually November)**: floating a krathong (decorated float) on water to pay respect to Phra Mae Khongkha (the water goddess) and let go of misfortune. Greeting tone is gentler/more reflective than Songkran's festive energy — common phrasing wishes the recipient's troubles "float away" with the krathong.

**New Year (ปีใหม่, 1 January)**: standard "สวัสดีปีใหม่" greetings; more universal/secular than Songkran or Loy Krathong.

**Taboo numbers**: the number 4 (สี่) is commonly avoided in Sino-Thai (Thai-Chinese) business and gift contexts — this comes from Chinese-language superstition (4 sounds like "death" in Chinese), carried into Thailand through its large Thai-Chinese population, not from the Thai language itself (สี่ doesn't sound like anything ominous in Thai). Still worth avoiding in amounts/quantities for gifts or red envelopes (อั่งเปา) when the audience has Chinese-Thai heritage or the context is commercial.

**Taboo colors**: black is associated with mourning — avoid as the dominant color for a celebratory card (New Year, wedding, birthday). White is reserved for the bride at a Thai wedding; avoid dressing a wedding-congratulations card entirely in white if it could read as competing with bridal symbolism. Some traditions follow a "color of the day" system (Monday=yellow, Tuesday=pink, Wednesday=green, Thursday=orange, Friday=blue, Saturday=purple, Sunday=red) tied to the recipient's birth day — a nice-to-have personalization for a birthday card, not a strict requirement.

**Taboo gifts**:
- นาฬิกา (a clock/watch) — avoid; "giving a clock" carries a Chinese-derived association with wishing someone's time to run out, same root as the number-4 taboo
- ผ้าเช็ดหน้า (a handkerchief) — avoid, especially as a romantic gift; associated with tears/parting
- ของมีคม (sharp objects — knives, scissors) — avoid; symbolizes "cutting" a relationship
- รองเท้า (shoes) — avoid as a romantic gift in some traditions; associated with someone "walking away"
- White flowers in certain arrangements are associated with funerals — check the specific flower/arrangement context before using in a celebratory bouquet suggestion

**Condolence messages (คำไว้อาลัย)**: register is formal and restrained, not effusive. Common phrasing expresses sympathy for the loss and support for the family, avoids festive language entirely, and typically does not use exclamation points or bright/celebratory phrasing. Color context for condolence cards/wreaths is black/white, not festival colors.

## Common mistakes

1. Suggesting a clock, handkerchief, or sharp object as a gift without flagging the taboo association.
2. Using black as a card's dominant color for a celebratory occasion.
3. Writing a Songkran greeting to an elder in the same festive/water-fight tone appropriate for peers, missing the รดน้ำดำหัว respect register.
4. Writing a condolence message with festive-adjacent phrasing or exclamation points.
5. Treating the number-4 taboo as a universal Thai-language superstition rather than what it actually is — a Sino-Thai commercial custom — and over-applying it in a context where it doesn't fit (e.g. it has no bearing on, say, a house address in a non-commercial personal context, though many people avoid it there too out of general caution).
6. Dressing a wedding congratulations card entirely in white, competing with the bride's traditional color.

## Known limitations

- Taboo strength varies by generation, region, and how much a given family follows traditional/Chinese-influenced customs — treat this as "worth flagging," not an absolute rule for every recipient. When the stakes are real (an actual gift, not a hypothetical), it's safer to flag the taboo and let the user decide than to silently avoid or silently ignore it.
- Regional and ethnic-minority Thai communities (e.g. Muslim-Thai in the south, various hill-tribe communities) have their own distinct greeting/gift customs not covered here — this skill reflects central-Thai, Buddhist-majority, Sino-Thai-influenced convention, the most common default, not the only one.
